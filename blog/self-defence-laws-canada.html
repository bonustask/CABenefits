Self-Defence Laws in Canada

Home invasions, reasonable force, weapons laws and real court cases. Test your knowledge first.

Can I defend myself?Yes. If you reasonably perceive a threat, act to protect someone and respond reasonably.
Can I protect my family?Yes. The same law protects other people, not just relatives.

The 3 rules of self-defence

Section 34 of the Criminal Code sets out the current test, in force since 2013. If all three requirements are met, the defensive act is legally justified — not merely grounds for a lighter sentence.

All three must apply (section 34)

  1. Reasonable belief in force or a threat. You must believe, on reasonable grounds, that someone is using or threatening force against you or another person. A genuine mistake can be relevant if it was reasonable; fear alone is not enough.
  2. A defensive purpose. The act must be intended to protect a person against that force or threat. Punishment, retaliation and revenge are not defensive purposes.
  3. A reasonable response. What you did must be reasonable considering the circumstances as a whole. The court considers the situation you faced, not simply whether you personally thought your actions were justified.

All three are necessary. The Supreme Court in R. v. Khill, 2021 SCC 37 describes them in terms of the catalyst, motive and response. The test applies even outside your home.

What courts look at: the threat, your options, your role, weapons, physical differences, relationship history, prior interactions, proportionality and whether the other person's force was lawful.

Section 34(3) also limits the defence against people carrying out authorized law-enforcement duties, unless there are reasonable grounds to believe they are acting unlawfully. Read the actual section 34 and the Justice Canada technical guide for the full legal language.

Does reasonable force mean equal force?

No. Canadian law does not prescribe a matching response for each kind of attack. Proportionality is a factor within a broader assessment, rather than an exact mathematical rule. Courts consider uncertainty, the seriousness of the danger and the other circumstances. At the same time, an unreasonable response is not excused just because an attacker initiated the incident.

Must an attack already have begun?

No. A credible threat may meet the first requirement without physical contact. Imminence is a factor, not an absolute precondition. Historical abuse, threatening communications and the circumstances surrounding an encounter can all be relevant. A vague fear of what somebody might do at an unknown time, however, is not a general licence to use violence.

Who has the burden of proof?

Once there is enough evidence to raise self-defence, the Crown must disprove at least one of its requirements beyond a reasonable doubt. The initial evidentiary threshold is called an air of reality. Evidence can come from either side. If the Crown fails to disprove the defence, the accused must be acquitted of the relevant offence. See the National Judicial Institute's model jury instructions.

Home invasions: what are your rights?

Canada does not have a blanket rule that residents must flee their home before defending themselves. Nor does entry into a dwelling automatically establish that every response is lawful. The home is a significant part of the factual context, and courts have recognized the common-law principle that a person need not abandon their home to an attacker.

Under today's section 34(2)(b), the timing of the danger and alternative ways of responding remain relevant to the reasonableness analysis. Canadian appellate decisions have addressed this issue in different factual settings. For example, R. v. Cunha discussed the home-retreat principle under the former law, while Cormier v. R. considered the availability of alternatives under the modern framework. The National Judicial Institute discusses these decisions. Do not confuse a safety recommendation to leave if possible with an absolute legal duty to do so.

The commonly used American terms castle doctrine and stand your ground describe laws that differ between US states. They are not substitutes for the Canadian statutory test. Canada has home-related common-law protections but not a blanket American-style immunity for residents who injure intruders.

Protecting a person versus protecting property

LawInterest protectedGeneral test
Section 34Personal safetyReasonably perceived force or threat, defensive purpose, reasonable act.
Section 35Peaceable possession of propertyReasonable belief in possession and specified interference, a property-protection purpose, and a reasonable act.
Section 494Limited citizen's arrest powersDifferent statutory conditions apply. Prompt delivery to police is required.

Section 35 can apply to preventing certain unauthorized entry, removing someone in appropriate circumstances, or preventing or reversing certain interference with property. It can also apply to someone assisting a person reasonably believed to be in peaceable possession. There are exceptions, including certain superior legal claims to possession and authorized law enforcement. A tenant, not just a homeowner, can have protected possession.

Justice Canada's guide to section 35 explains that deadly force is not reasonable merely to protect property. A theft involving danger to people may engage section 34 too, but the fact that something valuable is being stolen does not by itself make it a threat to life.

What if the intruder is leaving?

When a threat has ended, a continued attack is much harder to justify as protection. Do not pursue someone to recover belongings, punish them or settle the matter yourself. A person fleeing could still pose a threat in exceptional circumstances, but the assessment depends on the actual danger, not just their previous conduct.

Can I make a citizen's arrest?

Section 494 gives members of the public limited arrest powers, including certain circumstances involving offences found being committed on or in relation to property. An arrest under this section must be followed by delivery to a peace officer without delay. This is not a general power to detain anyone you suspect or to punish them. The relevant conditions and limits on force matter; attempting detention can create serious physical and legal risks. Let police handle it whenever possible.

What can you legally own or carry?

Owning something legally does not automatically make its use against a person legal. The law considers possession, the purpose of carrying an item and the circumstances of any use of force separately.

ItemLegal contextPractical takeaway
Phone or emergency alert deviceOrdinary communication equipment.Keep a way to call for help accessible.
Personal alarm, door alarm or security cameraGenerally lawful; privacy and building rules may apply.Used for alerting others, detection and evidence, not confrontation.
FlashlightOrdinary illumination equipment.Useful for visibility, power outages and safe evacuation.
Locks, secure doors and windowsGenerally lawful; tenancy, fire and building requirements apply.Reduce unauthorized access while keeping emergency exits usable.
Kitchen knives, sports equipment and other household objectsOrdinary lawful possession for their intended purpose is different from possession or use as a weapon.There is no special legal exemption for household objects. Avoid preparing or selecting objects for a confrontation.

FirearmsLicensing, classification, storage and use restrictions apply.Lawful ownership does not create automatic authority to use a firearm against someone.

The Criminal Code's definition of a weapon in section 2 includes items used, designed or intended to cause injury or to threaten or intimidate a person. Section 88 prohibits possession or carrying for a dangerous purpose or to commit an offence; section 90 addresses concealed weapons. The circumstances and intended use matter. See the section 88 text and prohibited weapons regulations.

Does having a firearm licence change your right to self-defence?

No. Section 34 does not make a different reasonableness test for licensed gun owners. A defensive act involving a firearm may be legally justified in exceptional circumstances, but licence conditions, storage and other firearm-related offences can raise separate legal issues. You may not assume that a firearms licence is a licence to carry for protection or to keep a firearm ready for a confrontation. Consult the RCMP's storage rules and firearms classification information.

What about traps on your property?

Do not install devices designed to injure intruders. Section 247 of the Criminal Code addresses traps likely to cause bodily harm. A hidden hazard may also injure a child, visitor, firefighter or emergency responder. Use lawful security measures that prevent entry or summon help instead.

What to do before, during and after a break-in

Before an emergency

  • Check exterior doors, windows and locks. Ask your landlord or building management to fix security problems if you rent.
  • Use lighting and alarms where suitable. Cameras may help document incidents; they are not a substitute for a safety plan.
  • Keep emergency contacts and a charged phone accessible. Make sure everyone knows the address.
  • Agree on a household emergency plan, including accessible exits and a safe meeting place. Account for children, older adults, pets and mobility needs.
  • Keep emergency exits usable. Security devices should comply with local building and fire rules.
  • Ask local police about home-security assessments or community prevention programs.

During an emergency

  • Get to a safer place if you can do so without increasing the danger. Call emergency services as soon as it is safe.
  • Give the dispatcher your location, whether anyone is injured, and information about immediate threats that you actually know. Follow their instructions.
  • Avoid confronting, following or attempting to apprehend suspects. Personal safety is more important than possessions.
  • If a safe exit is not available, put distance and a secure barrier between yourself and the danger if possible. Do not assume a particular plan is safe in every home.
  • When officers arrive, follow their safety instructions. Do not make sudden movements or approach officers holding objects.

After an emergency

  • Seek medical attention, including for injuries that may not initially seem serious. Follow emergency dispatch instructions.
  • Once safe, preserve original security recordings, messages, photographs and witness contact information. Do not alter or delete material or disturb the scene unnecessarily.
  • Do not publish evidence, accusations or speculation online. Discuss the incident with your lawyer if you face possible charges.
  • Contact your insurer and landlord if appropriate. Keep records of expenses, repairs and lost property.
  • Ask about victim services, counselling and emergency financial support. Fear, sleep disruption and stress can persist after the event.

For more prevention guidance, see Toronto Police home security advice and break-and-enter prevention guidance.

Real Canadian cases and outcomes

These examples include both reported prosecution decisions and published appellate judgments. They are not all home invasions. A withdrawn charge is not a judgment establishing a legal precedent; a decision ordering a retrial does not itself determine guilt or innocence.

Ontario · February 26, 2026 · Charges withdrawn

Jeremy David McDonald — Lindsay apartment break-in

Charges withdrawn — The Crown discontinued the homeowner’s case in February 2026. No new court precedent was created.

Read case details and sources

In August 2025, McDonald was accused of assaulting an intruder in his apartment. Prosecutors subsequently considered medical and forensic evidence alongside the self-defence provisions. On February 26, 2026, the Crown withdrew the charges after determining that the evidence supporting the defence meant conviction was no longer reasonably likely.

The intruder, Michael Kyle Breen, subsequently pleaded guilty to breaking and entering and breach of probation in April 2026. Lesson: charging and prosecuting are distinct stages; a resident can be investigated and later have the case discontinued. No trial verdict established a new self-defence rule here.

Ali Mian — Milton home invasion

Murder charge withdrawn — The 2023 charge was withdrawn. No court ruled that every similar use of force is lawful.

Mian was charged with second-degree murder following the death of a person during a reported home invasion in February 2023. His lawyer maintained that Mian was defending himself and his mother. The murder charge was withdrawn in July 2023.

Lesson: even a serious charge can be discontinued as evidence is assessed. This was not a court ruling that a firearm may automatically be used against any intruder.

Supreme Court of Canada · 2021; subsequent proceedings through 2025

R. v. Khill, 2021 SCC 37

Manslaughter conviction · 6 years — An initial acquittal was overturned. The retrial ended in conviction; the sentence was reduced on appeal.

Read case details and sources

Peter Khill fatally shot Jon Styres during a confrontation outside Khill's home involving a suspected theft from his vehicle in 2016. Khill claimed self-defence. This case is important because it examines the resident's own conduct leading up to a confrontation, not just the final moment.

  • 2018: jury acquitted Khill.
  • 2020: Ontario Court of Appeal overturned the acquittal and ordered a new trial.
  • October 14, 2021: Supreme Court upheld the order for a new trial. The jury instructions had inadequately addressed Khill's role in the incident.
  • December 2022: convicted of manslaughter at retrial.
  • June 2023: sentenced to eight years; February 27, 2025: Ontario Court of Appeal upheld the conviction and reduced the sentence to six years (2025 ONCA 146).
  • July 31, 2025: Supreme Court denied leave to appeal.

Lesson: an initial acquittal is not necessarily the final outcome. The law considers whether the response and the accused's conduct connected to the incident were reasonable.

Ontario Court of Appeal · 2016 · New trial ordered

R. v. Cunha, 2016 ONCA 491

New trial ordered — The appeal court found problems with the trial’s assessment. Final retrial outcome not verified.

Read case details and sources

After a confrontation in his home, Valter Cunha was convicted despite a claim of self-defence. The Court of Appeal concluded that the trial assessment did not properly account for the circumstances from the resident's perspective and ordered a new trial. The incident preceded the modern provisions, though the appeal court noted that the statutory changes did not affect the relevant analysis in this case.

Lesson: a person's perception of a threatening encounter at home matters, and courts must assess that perception in context. The appeal was not itself a final acquittal. The ultimate retrial outcome has not been verified for this guide.

New Brunswick Court of Appeal · 2017 · New trial ordered

Cormier v. R., 2017 NBCA 10

New trial ordered — The appeal dealt with self-defence instructions and alternatives to force. Final retrial outcome not verified.

Read case details and sources

The appeal court set aside a conviction and ordered a new trial after considering problems with the self-defence instructions. Its discussion of the current law distinguished an obligatory retreat from the consideration of alternative responses. The decision is relevant when a person is defending themselves or assisting another in a home.

Lesson: the existence of possible alternatives is part of the contextual analysis, not a universal requirement to flee. A new trial is not an acquittal; a later disposition has not been verified here.

National Judicial Institute discussion of Cormier · Dalhousie legal case commentary

Supreme Court of Canada · July 12, 2024 · Acquittal restored

R. v. Hodgson, 2024 SCC 25

Acquittal restored — The Supreme Court found no legal error justifying a new trial.

Read case details and sources

A confrontation while removing a guest from a house party resulted in a death. The trial judge acquitted Hodgson of murder and manslaughter; the latter finding involved section 34. The Nunavut Court of Appeal ordered another trial. The Supreme Court restored the acquittal because it identified no legal error justifying appellate intervention.

Lesson: a court must apply the three statutory requirements to the evidence. The Crown cannot overturn an acquittal simply because it disagrees with factual findings. This is not a general approval of dangerous restraints or other force.

Supreme Court of Canada · 1990 · Acquittal restored

R. v. Lavallee, [1990] 1 S.C.R. 852

Acquittal restored — The Supreme Court recognized the importance of prior abuse and expert evidence.

Read case details and sources

This landmark decision involved a woman who had experienced prolonged intimate partner violence and was prosecuted for killing her abusive partner. The Supreme Court restored the acquittal and recognized the value of expert evidence in explaining how abuse can affect someone's understanding of danger.

Lesson: courts should not assess threats through stereotypes about how someone experiencing abuse ought to behave. This case predates the current law, but the context of prior violence is now expressly relevant under section 34(2)(f).

Supreme Court of Canada · 2002 · Conviction restored

R. v. Cinous, 2002 SCC 29

Murder conviction restored — The claimed defence lacked a sufficient evidentiary basis under the former law.

Read case details and sources

Cinous claimed self-defence in a killing connected to criminal associates. The Supreme Court found that the defence did not have a sufficient evidentiary basis under the law then in force and restored the murder conviction.

Lesson: an assertion of fear is not necessarily enough to put self-defence before a jury. The air-of-reality principle remains important, although the former substantive self-defence test should not be substituted for today's section 34.

What happens if police charge you?

You can be the victim of a break-in and still be investigated for a separate alleged offence. Police may not have the full story at first. Here is how a criminal case can unfold.

  1. Emergency response and investigation. Police secure the scene, obtain evidence and arrange medical assistance. They may interview witnesses, gather video and seize relevant items lawfully.
  2. Arrest, detention or release. An arrest is subject to legal grounds and does not establish guilt. Some provinces have pre-charge Crown approval processes; in others police generally lay charges before Crown screening.
  3. Crown screening. Prosecutors assess whether the case should continue. In Ontario, both a reasonable prospect of conviction and the public interest are required. The assessment continues as new information becomes available.
  4. Bail or other release conditions. If charged, an accused may be released by police or the court, with conditions where applicable, or held pending a hearing. Bail concerns interim release, not the verdict.
  5. Disclosure and legal preparation. Defence counsel reviews the Crown's disclosure, assesses the evidence, identifies applicable defences and advises on how the case should proceed.
  6. Resolution or trial. Charges may be withdrawn or stayed, a plea may resolve a case, or the matter may go to trial. If self-defence is properly raised, the Crown has the burden described above.
  7. Verdict, possible sentence and appeals. An acquittal means the accused is not found guilty. A conviction can lead to sentencing. Appeal courts may intervene on applicable legal grounds.

Read Ontario's Crown charge-screening policy and Legal Aid Ontario's criminal process guide. Other jurisdictions may use differently worded prosecution standards.

What criminal charges could arise?

Depending on the alleged conduct, charges could include assault (section 266), assault with a weapon or causing bodily harm (267), aggravated assault (268), weapons offences, manslaughter or murder. These are examples, not a prediction about any specific incident. A successful justification under section 34 may defeat an allegation relating to the defensive act. Independent conduct, such as earlier unlawful weapons possession, must be examined separately.

Your rights when arrested or detained

Section 10 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms entitles people who are arrested or detained to be informed promptly of the reasons and to retain and instruct counsel without delay. You generally have a right to remain silent, subject to specific legal obligations. You can ask whether you are being detained and request a lawyer. Do not physically resist police or provide false information.

In an emergency, tell responders what they need to know for immediate safety and medical care. If you are treated as a suspect, seek legal advice before giving a detailed account of the incident. Ask about duty counsel if you cannot afford private legal assistance. Justice Canada: right to counsel.

Can an intruder sue a resident?

A civil claim is distinct from criminal proceedings. The ability to file a lawsuit does not establish entitlement to damages. Provincial tort law (or Quebec civil law), justification, fault, injuries and other facts may determine liability. Even where charges are not brought or are withdrawn, criminal and civil standards differ. Seek advice from a lawyer in your province, and notify your insurer as required by your policy.

Recent home invasions and proposed laws

On November 25, 2026, Toronto musician Michael Eckert died following a home invasion in the city's Casa Loma neighbourhood. The case was still being investigated when this article was prepared. CityNews coverage.

Earlier in the month, Toronto Police reported an arrest in connection with a violent October 29 home invasion in the Bridle Path area. The police continued investigating and looking for another suspect. These are allegations, not findings of guilt. Toronto Police update, November 16.

November 27, 2026: Ontario asks Ottawa to change the law

Ontario's Attorney General and Solicitor General requested federal reforms addressing home invasions, including an express assurance that residents need not leave their homes before relying on self-defence, clearer consideration of the circumstances of an invasion and guidance to improve how cases are assessed. The existing Criminal Code already recognizes self-defence; the request is for additional clarity and protections. Canadian Press reporting via Global News.

Bill C-270: the proposed Stand on Guard Act

Legislative status as checked November 28, 2026: proposed, not law. Parliament's official record shows Bill C-270 at second reading in the House of Commons, with debate on November 19. It has not reached committee or the Senate. Check the current status.

Introduced March 12, 2026, the private member's bill would add presumptions to section 34 relating to unlawful entry into a dwelling where a person is lawfully present. Its text addresses defensive force, including lethal force, and would change how certain facts are presumed in these cases. Read the bill itself, rather than assuming its title describes an existing legal right.

The policy debate involves two concerns: whether current law gives residents enough clarity and protection from unnecessary prosecution, and whether broader presumptions could excuse unreasonable violence in cases of mistaken identity or unusual circumstances. The existing test remains in effect unless and until Parliament changes it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I legally defend myself in Canada?

Yes. Section 34 applies when you reasonably perceive force or a threat, act to protect yourself or another person, and respond reasonably in the circumstances.

Can I defend my spouse, child or stranger?

Yes. Section 34 includes defending other people. The same three requirements apply, whether or not you are related to the person.

Does an intruder need to have a weapon before self-defence applies?

No. Weapons are one factor. An unarmed person can present a serious threat, depending on the facts.

Do I need to be hit first?

No. A reasonably perceived threat of force can qualify. Timing and available alternatives are relevant to whether the response was reasonable.

Do I have to run away from my home?

There is no blanket duty to retreat before defending yourself. Available options may still matter under section 34. Leaving safely is a safety choice, not an admission that you have no right to defend yourself.

Can I use deadly force against a home intruder?

It is not automatically lawful. In exceptional cases it may be justified under section 34 if all three statutory requirements are satisfied. Deadly force to protect property alone is not reasonable.

Is pepper spray legal for self-defence?

Sprays designed to incapacitate people are prohibited weapons. Animal deterrents are legally distinct but are not a general legal substitute for human-directed pepper spray.

Can I carry a knife for protection?

Some knives are prohibited regardless of purpose. Other knives may be lawful for ordinary activities, but carrying an item as a weapon can engage weapons laws, including sections 88 and 90. There is no blanket right to carry a knife for protection.

Can I use something already in my home?

Ordinary objects can become weapons under the Code depending on use or intent. There is no universal list of approved defensive objects. Any defensive act is assessed under the applicable law.

What if the intruder is a family member or someone I know?

Section 34 can apply regardless of relationship. Prior abuse and communications can help explain a reasonable perception of danger. If violence is ongoing, contact emergency services or a local family-violence service when safe.

Can I be arrested even if I acted in self-defence?

Yes, if police have the applicable legal grounds. Investigations and Crown reviews can later result in charges being withdrawn or a court may acquit. An arrest does not prove guilt.

Do renters have the same right to defend themselves?

Yes. Section 34 does not depend on owning the home. Section 35 concerns peaceable possession, which can include rented property.

Can I chase someone who stole my car?

Police advise against confronting suspects. A theft by itself does not justify violence against a person, and deadly force cannot be justified solely to recover property. Report the theft and preserve evidence.

Is Bill C-270 already in effect?

No. As of November 28, 2026, the bill was at second reading. Check Parliament's official status page for subsequent changes.

Does self-defence law differ between Ontario, Quebec and other provinces?

The Criminal Code test is federal. Prosecution policies, legal aid, victim services, policing and civil liability can differ by jurisdiction.

Do I need to prove I acted in self-defence?

Once the defence has an adequate evidentiary foundation, the Crown must disprove at least one of the statutory requirements beyond a reasonable doubt. Consult counsel if you are being investigated or charged.

The bottom line

Canadians have the right to defend themselves and others. That right is not dependent on owning a home, being injured first, or matching the attacker's force exactly. But it is tied to reasonable grounds, a protective purpose and a reasonable response. Prioritize getting people to safety, seek emergency assistance and obtain legal advice if an incident leads to an investigation.

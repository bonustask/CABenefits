Cheapest Places to Rent in Canada (November 2026)

Updated November 26, 2026

If rent is eating most of your income, moving somewhere cheaper might make a real difference. But a cheap apartment is only useful if you can afford to live there, find work, access health care and keep your support network. This guide compares asking rents across Canada, shows where to apply for subsidized housing, and tracks what governments are actually funding or building.

Averages do not guarantee a vacancy or a suitable unit.

Renting a house versus an apartment

Nationally, advertised rents for secondary-market properties other than condos (including houses and townhouses) averaged $2,016, compared with $2,040 for purpose-built apartments and $2,048 for condos. This does not mean a detached house is cheaper than a comparable apartment; size, location and the mix of available units differ. Four-bedroom-or-larger units across all property types averaged $2,880. Compare full homes separately from basement suites and rooms, and ask who pays utilities.

Rooms and shared accommodation

A room can be a temporary lower-cost option for a single renter. The reported four-province room averages were Alberta $809, Quebec $812, Ontario $1,002 and BC $1,013. Check whether you have a tenancy agreement, whether the landlord shares the kitchen or bathroom, and what rights apply in your province.

Source: Rentals.ca November 2026 report.

Subsidized housing is not the same as a cheap market rental. Rent-geared-to-income (RGI) generally adjusts rent based on eligible household income; other affordable housing may charge a fixed below-market rent. Quebec's HLM program generally uses 25% of income, while many other RGI programs use around 30%, subject to local formulas, minimum rents and utilities.

Statistics Canada's household survey estimates the number of households reporting someone on a waitlist. Administrative registries use different dates, eligibility criteria and coverage, and can count applicants across multiple lists. Do not add the numbers below or compare them directly to the national survey estimate. Time already spent waiting is not a promise of time until placement.

Who gets priority? There is no national queue. Local programs may consider immediate safety risks, homelessness, severe housing need, accessibility, household size, income, age, local residency and application date. Being on disability or social assistance does not automatically move everyone to the front. If you are fleeing abuse, have no safe place to live or need an accessible unit, tell the housing provider and ask about the formal priority process. Keep your file updated and ask whether applications to additional providers are allowed.

Ontario permits applications to more than one regional waiting list, while BC's registry covers participating providers and other providers accept separate applications. Other jurisdictions may impose residency or local eligibility rules. Do not move provinces solely to join a list without confirming those rules, and check how social assistance, health coverage or other benefits would transfer.

Before moving to a cheaper city

  1. Search actual listings. Compare like-for-like units, lease dates, deposits, utilities and transit. Never send money before verifying the landlord and rental.
  2. Check income and employment. Compare local job opportunities, wages and what happens to your benefits if you move provinces.
  3. Check services. Family doctors, specialists, schools, child care, pharmacies, accessible transportation and community supports may be harder to access in small or remote communities.
  4. Apply for subsidized housing early. Ask about additional providers, priority applications, accessible units, co-ops and rent supplements. Keep your contact details and documents current.
  5. Plan the full cost. Budget for transport, rent overlap, deposits where legal, moving, furnishings, heating and a contingency. Do not give up existing housing until you have a confirmed safe alternative.
Need somewhere safe right now? A housing waitlist is not emergency shelter. Call 211 where available to find emergency housing, rent banks or local crisis supports. Call 911 if you are in immediate danger. For confidential safety planning, contact a local domestic violence service or shelter. You do not have to wait for subsidized housing before seeking immediate help.

Frequently asked questions

What is the cheapest city to rent in Canada?

Among 60 markets in the October 2026 Rentals.ca report, Lloydminster has the lowest overall asking rent ($1,319). Fort McMurray has the lowest average one-bedroom asking rent ($1,140). Smaller towns outside the report could be cheaper.

What is the cheapest province for renters?

Saskatchewan has the lowest average asking rent for apartments and condos among the seven provinces separately published in the October report ($1,380). The answer changes if you look at existing occupied rental stock instead.

Can I get a subsidized apartment immediately?

Usually not. Ask the housing authority about local availability, priority eligibility and emergency or transitional housing. Waitlist lengths can change considerably by building and household type.

Can I apply to multiple subsidized housing waitlists?

In Ontario you can apply to multiple local service areas; BC also has providers outside its registry. Elsewhere, check residency and provider rules before applying. Avoid duplicate applications to the same provider.

Does more affordable housing construction mean shorter waits?

Not necessarily. Units may still be funded but not built; a building may have different income limits, designated tenants or rents above your budget. Demand and turnover also affect placement.

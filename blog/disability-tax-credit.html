Disability Tax Credit: which conditions qualify, and how to apply

What the DTC is worth in 2026, how the CRA decides, and answers to the questions people ask most.

Updated November 26, 2026 · About 10 minutes to read

The Disability Tax Credit (DTC) is one of the most valuable — and most misunderstood — programs for people with disabilities in Canada. The biggest surprise for most people: almost no condition qualifies or fails automatically. Apart from a few exceptions like blindness and type 1 diabetes, the CRA looks at how your condition affects everyday activities, not at your diagnosis.

Quick answers
  • Does ADHD, autism, anxiety, depression or chronic pain qualify? They can — if the effects are severe enough, most of the time. See the conditions table below, or search 1,000+ conditions.
  • Is it a monthly cheque? No. It lowers the income tax you owe. But approval unlocks programs that do pay, including the Canada Disability Benefit and the RDSP.
  • Can I get back pay? Yes. If you’re approved for past years, the CRA can adjust up to 10 years of tax returns.
  • Do I need to pay a company to apply? No. Applying to the CRA is free.

Because the DTC is non-refundable, it only helps if someone owes income tax. If you don’t earn enough to pay tax, a supporting family member — such as a parent, spouse or adult child who helps with food, shelter or clothing — may be able to claim the unused part.

Why it’s worth applying even if you pay no tax

DTC approval is the key that opens other programs:

  • Canada Disability Benefit — up to $204.20 a month (July 2026 to June 2027) for eligible adults aged 18 to 64 with low income. You must be DTC-approved and file your taxes.
  • Registered Disability Savings Plan (RDSP) — a savings plan where the government adds grants and bonds, even if you can’t contribute much.
  • Child Disability Benefit — an extra monthly amount added to the Canada Child Benefit for each DTC-approved child.
  • Canada Workers Benefit disability supplement — extra money for low-income workers.

Who qualifies: the three tests

To qualify, your impairment has to be:

  1. Prolonged — it has lasted, or is expected to last, at least 12 months in a row.
  2. Severe — you are “markedly restricted” in at least one basic activity of daily living, even with therapy, medication and devices. That means you can’t do it, or it takes you an “inordinate amount of time” (generally about three times longer than someone your age without the impairment).
  3. Present nearly all the time — the restriction happens all or substantially all of the time, which the CRA generally treats as at least 90% of the time.

There are two other routes: cumulative effect (significant restrictions in two or more activities that together equal one marked restriction) and life-sustaining therapy (therapy at least twice a week, averaging 14 hours or more per week).

What doesn’t count Your ability to work, do housework, or take part in social or recreational activities is not part of the test. Being approved for CPP disability, ODSP, AISH, workers’ compensation or insurance benefits doesn’t mean automatic DTC approval either — those programs use different rules.

Which conditions can qualify?

This is the question people ask most. The honest answer is “it depends on the effects” — but here’s how common conditions usually fit. None of these are automatic except where noted.

If your symptoms come and go Many conditions have good and bad days — MS, Crohn’s, lupus, migraine, endometriosis, mental health conditions and chronic pain among them. The CRA still needs the restriction to be present at least 90% of the time, so:
  • Describe your usual day, not just flare-ups. If you’re limited most days, say so, and say what it stops you from doing.
  • Look at cumulative effect. One condition often affects several areas at once — for example walking, bowel or bladder function, and concentration or sleep. Significant limits in two or more areas can qualify together.
  • Keep a simple diary for a few months: how many days you’re limited, how long tasks take, and what help you need.
  • List the treatments you’ve tried to show the limits remain even with treatment.
Tax Court decisions have turned on this point: severe symptoms that cluster on some days of the month, without enough limitation the rest of the time, have not met the test.

How to apply, step by step

  1. Fill out Part A of Form T2201 — online in your CRA account (Benefits and credits › Apply for DTC) or by phone at 1-800-959-8281. You’ll get a reference number that’s valid for 12 months.
  2. Give the reference number to your practitioner. They complete Part B in the CRA’s digital application, and it’s sent automatically. (You can also use the paper T2201 and mail both parts together.)
  3. Agree to have past years adjusted. In Part A, you can let the CRA automatically reassess earlier tax years if you’re approved.
  4. Wait for a decision. Many decisions take up to about 16 weeks. The CRA sometimes sends your practitioner a follow-up questionnaire — ask them to answer it quickly, because no answer can mean a denial.
  5. Read your notice of determination. It shows which years you’re approved for and whether your approval expires.
Tips that make the biggest difference
  • Talk about daily life, not the diagnosis. Tell your practitioner how long things take, how often you need help, and what happens on a typical day.
  • Use comparisons. “Getting dressed takes me 45 minutes and I need help with buttons” is stronger than “dressing is hard.”
  • Leave out work, driving, banking, housework and hobbies — they aren’t part of the test.
  • No family doctor? Walk-in clinics, virtual-care doctors, nurse practitioners and specialists (like psychologists or physiotherapists) can certify the sections that match their field.
  • Practitioner fees (often $50–$300) can be claimed as a medical expense. Ask if they offer a reduced fee.
Be careful with DTC companies Some companies offer to apply for you in exchange for a large share of your refund. You don’t need them — the CRA charges nothing, and free help is available from tax clinics run through the CRA’s Community Volunteer Income Tax Program, disability organizations and advocates. Never sign anything that gives a company access to your CRA account or refund without reading it carefully.

If you’re denied

A denial often means the form was missing information, not that you don’t qualify. Your notice explains why. You can:

  • Ask for a review by sending new information — such as a letter from your practitioner or updated medical reports — through your CRA account or by mail.
  • File a formal objection within 90 days of the notice of determination.
  • Reapply with a new, more detailed form. There’s no waiting period and no limit on how many times you can apply.

Common questions

How much back pay could I get?

It depends on how much tax you paid in each year you’re approved for. If you paid income tax every year, being approved for several past years can add up to thousands of dollars. If you paid little or no tax, the refund may be small — but approval still unlocks the RDSP, the Canada Disability Benefit and the Child Disability Benefit.

My doctor says I won’t qualify. What can I do?

Ask which category they considered, and whether they looked at cumulative effect. You can also ask a different eligible practitioner — for example, a psychologist for mental functions or an occupational therapist for walking, feeding and dressing.

Can I apply for my child?

Yes. There’s no minimum age. Children are compared with others their own age, and parents usually claim the amount. Approval also adds the Child Disability Benefit to your Canada Child Benefit.

Do I need to reapply every year?

No. Approvals can last a few years or have no expiry date. If yours expires, the CRA reminds you a year ahead. You do have to tell the CRA if your condition significantly improves.

Does getting CPP disability or provincial disability assistance mean I qualify?

No — the programs use different rules. Many people approved for one are eligible for the other, but you still need a separate DTC application.

Will the DTC or the Canada Disability Benefit affect my provincial disability assistance?

The rules differ by province and by benefit. Ask your caseworker before you apply so there are no surprises.

My pain is constant but gets much worse at times. Will I qualify?

You might, if the pain markedly restricts walking, eliminating, dressing, feeding or mental functions at least 90% of the time — or significantly restricts two or more of those together. Applications are strongest when they describe a typical week, not only the worst days, and show the limits continue even with treatment.

Do I have to have a diagnosis?

No. The test is about the effects of your impairment. A diagnosis helps your practitioner explain those effects, but it isn’t the deciding factor.
